# Weather

The wind changes with the weather: settled days, trade winds, unsettled and stormy spells, and systems that cross the disk. Showers and squalls are cells under a mile across. A storm turns round the outer waters with a gale in its ring; a low is a storm at half its strength. A squall line is squalls in a row miles long, its gust front all along it; a trough a line of showers drifting with the wind. A cold front brings its rain behind its line, a squall line often ahead of it; a warm front its rain ahead, and the wind veers as it passes. Fog banks and patches of light air come in calm weather.

The report's Weather line has the barometer and its tendency over a quarter of an hour ("1009 hPa, falling quickly": weather coming), the visibility, rain, and the clouds about. Thunder and fog signals are on the Hear line.

`forecast` is the truth for the next 90 minutes, as every sailor's chart shows it: gale warnings in force, every system with its wind and force now, where it is, which way it moves, and where it will be in 30, 60 and 90 minutes, and each front, warm or cold, and when it reaches you. Only the gusts and the wind's shifts are not forecast. To meet a storm, steer for where it will be. `forecast` with `place` gives one charted place's box and outlook, as its harbourmaster's board does.

A squall brings a sudden strong wind and a shift: ease the sheet before it arrives. A gale warning means force 8 or more: in a small boat, reef by easing, run for shelter, or keep away.

In fog, the visibility falls to a few hundred metres: islands and boats are seen late. Lighthouses sound fog signals.
